Chunk assembly guide

Every printed piece of this boat carries a number. This page says what each number is, where it sits in the hull, what it bonds to, and — the part that costs real parts when it goes wrong — which of two identical-looking halves you are holding.

How the numbering works

The four transportable pieces are each cut on their own grid of printer-bed-sized chunks. A chunk's grid address is written x·y·z and appears in its filename, so 031_bow_x0y2z2.stl is chunk #031, at grid x0 y2 z2 of the bow.

  • x runs aft to forward. x0 is the aft end, at the x=60 joint; the highest x is the nose or the forward end of that piece.
  • y runs port to starboard. Low y is port, high y is starboard, and the outermost rows are the outboard chunks.
  • z stacks bottom to top. z0 sits on the keel, the highest z reaches the sheer.

Numbers run in grid order across the whole boat, so the bow is #001–#044, the center barge #045–#109, and the two wedges #110–#149. They are not a print order — print order is a separate question, and lives with the build notes rather than here.

Mirror twins, and why they matter

The hull is symmetrical, so nearly every chunk has a mirror twin on the other side of the centreline: the same solid, reflected. In the boards below a twin is always the reflection of its partner across the dashed line.

Size identifies the pair, never which half you are holding. Twins match to 0.000 mm on the bounding box and to within 0.04% on volume. A caliper, a scale and the manifest all read identical on both; only handedness differs. Once a twin leaves the print bed unlabelled, nothing in the toolchain can recover which one it is — both are watertight, both are manifest-correct, both are perfect prints.

So label every chunk in marker before it leaves the plate. It is the one defect on this boat that survives every automated check, and it has already happened.

Two kinds of exception are worth knowing, and both are marked below. A handful of pairs carry an asymmetric feature — a notch, a flange, a boss that exists on only one side — and those really do differ in volume, by up to 3%. For that handful, and only that handful, a scale separates the halves. Every other pair on the boat is identical to within measurement noise.

The other exception is structural. The center barge has an odd number of rows, so its middle row sits on the centreline and is its own reflection — those chunks are marked unique below and are unambiguous on sight. And the two wedges mirror each other rather than themselves, so a port wedge chunk's twin is a starboard wedge chunk, in a different piece entirely.

Seams and dowels

Every seam between two chunks is bonded and pinned. The dowel count in each chunk's bonds to list is the number of alignment pins on that one seam — a #013(3) entry means three dowels into chunk #013.

Dowels are printed: 4.0 mm pins into 4.2 mm bores, 6 mm into each side. That is 0.2 mm of total clearance, so a fit measured on one printer is only an assumption on another — dry-fit a dowel from each machine before any glue goes on. If they bind, do not open the holes. Opening a bore changes the spec for every chunk already printed; reprint the dowel smaller instead.

The inside bores figure on each chunk is the downward-facing area that belongs to a horizontal dowel bore rather than to a real overhang. On most chunks it is the majority of what a slicer reports as overhang, which is why supports — where they are used at all — must be set to build-plate-only. A support left on "everywhere" grows up into the bores, and a bore with support material in it cannot be cleaned without reaming it oversize.

Reading the print figures

Bed contact is the flat area actually touching the plate in the orientation the chunk is exported in. Ratio is height divided by that contact, and it is the single best predictor of a chunk letting go mid-print: the tall, narrow-footed chunks peel as the ASA shrinks, and the force grows the whole way up. Anything above roughly 10 wants a raft and the widest brim its plate position allows.

Stands on names the face the chunk was exported sitting on. Do not auto-orient in the slicer and do not rotate a chunk back to look like the boat — the exported stance is chosen to put a flat cut face on the plate.
